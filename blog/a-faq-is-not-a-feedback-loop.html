A FAQ is not a feedback loop.

Published October 8, 2026

A page that answers yesterday’s question is documentation. It may be useful documentation. But a team only has a learning system when the next person can inspect the answer, correct it after the work changes, and leave a usable trace for the person after them.

That distinction matters most in onboarding. A newcomer receives a clean answer, follows it once, and everyone declares victory. Then the procedure moves, the exception arrives, or the answer reaches a different team. If the only repair path is “ask the person who knows,” the FAQ was a pause button, not institutional memory.

Run the feedback-loop test

Choose one answer your team repeats and ask four unfashionably practical questions:

  1. Can a reader inspect the source? The answer should lead back to the procedure, decision, file, or record that supports it.
  2. Can they tell where it applies? A rule for one workspace, customer, team, or workflow should not quietly become permission everywhere else.
  3. Can they correct it without creating a second unofficial version? A correction needs a known home and a human review path.
  4. Does the next use start with the lesson? The improvement should be reachable when the comparable question returns—not stranded in a private chat.

Static answers still have a job

This is not an argument against FAQs. A concise answer is often the right front door. The mistake is treating the front door as the whole building. The durable layer sits behind it: the sources, the scope, and the place where a completed piece of work becomes a reviewed lesson.

That is also why adding more prose does not fix a weak knowledge system. A longer answer without a source is merely a longer assertion. A carefully designed answer with no correction path is a future support ticket wearing a nicer shirt.

Make the loop visible

My answer-that-survives card names the object: question, source, access boundary, and reviewed update. The second-use receipt checks what happens when someone relies on it. This final move is simpler: treat a useful answer as the start of a loop, not the end of a conversation.

Usable is built for the governed part of that loop: owned workspaces for procedures, decisions, files, and reusable context; access boundaries for the people and approved tools that may use them; source-returning retrieval; and solution writeback for lessons worth keeping. It is a practical way to give a repeated answer a home beyond chat history.
