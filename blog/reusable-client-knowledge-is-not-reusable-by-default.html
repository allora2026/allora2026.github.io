Reusable client knowledge is not reusable by default.

Published October 29, 2026

A consultancy learns in two directions at once. It discovers a better way to deliver the work, and it accumulates context about the client it is delivering for. The first may deserve to travel. The second may need to remain exactly where it earned its meaning.

That distinction gets flattened when an engagement closes. One bad answer traps every lesson in the project archive. The other copies client context into a general playbook because it looks useful. Neither is reuse. One wastes learning; the other mistakes possession for permission.

Ask one question before calling a lesson reusable

Remove the client name. What remains?

If the answer is a delivery standard, a reviewed implementation pattern, or a way of working that holds without the account-specific facts, it may be worth carrying forward. If it only makes sense with a client’s decision history, priorities, or working context, it is not suddenly general knowledge because the project has changed hands.

This is not a word game. It changes what the next team can safely retrieve. A useful handover needs both sides of the distinction: the scoped context that lets the current work continue, and the reviewed lesson that can improve the next engagement without smuggling the last one into it.

The archive cannot make that decision for you

A folder can keep both kinds of material. It cannot explain which team may use it next, which source still governs, or whether a lesson has been reviewed for reuse. That is why “we saved everything” is weaker than it sounds. Preservation is not an access rule; storage is not a delivery standard.

The practical move is small. Take one closeout note and label three things: the client-specific context that stays scoped, the delivery lesson that deserves review, and the question that still lacks enough evidence to become either. If the sort is difficult, that is not paperwork failing. It is the workflow confessing that its boundary was never clear.

Make the distinction usable

Usable publicly describes workspaces for knowledge separated by customer or project, access boundaries for who may use it, retrieval that brings the source back, and solution writeback for reviewed implementation lessons. That is the shape this distinction needs: not a magical classifier, and not a promise that a tool replaces delivery judgment, but a governed place to keep context scoped and carry forward the lesson that has actually earned reuse.

Pick one client handover that still requires someone to narrate what can travel and what must stay put. If the answer is buried in a project archive, explore how Usable gives approved people and AI tools shared memory inside the right boundary.
